1. Executive summary

This project designs, validates, and analyzes a daily market-timing strategy on the SPDR S&P 500 ETF (SPY). After each market close we forecast the next holding day’s excess return from twelve technical features — short-horizon momentum/reversal, realized volatility, moving-average deviations, RSI, intraday range, and volume change — using a ridge regression whose shrinkage penalty is selected on a strictly later validation window. The forecast is converted into a volatility-managed position, πt = clip(ŷt/(γσ̂t2), −1.5, 1.5), executed at the next day’s open — never at the same close that produced the signal. Uninvested capital earns the T-bill rate, leverage borrows at it, and trades pay 1 basis point per unit of turnover.

The procedure is built around leakage control and honest evaluation. Features use only information known at the decision close; standardization is fitted on the training window and applied forward; the shrinkage penalty is chosen on 2015–2019 validation data from a small pre-set grid of ten values; and the frozen configuration is then evaluated once on 2020–2026. Diagnostics computed after seeing the test results (cost/cap/long-only variants, a fixed-exposure benchmark, CAPM attribution) are reported separately as exploratory analysis and were not used to re-select anything.

Headline out-of-sample results (2020-01-02 to 2026-10-28, 1,715 trading days, net of costs): the ridge strategy earns 10.5% annualized with 12.3% volatility, a Sharpe ratio of 0.64, and a maximum drawdown of −14.1%, versus 15.1% / 19.1% / 0.69 / −32.1% for buy-and-hold SPY. It clearly beats an AR(1) forecast through the identical position rule (Sharpe 0.42, which loses 1.75% per year to costs). Attribution, however, tempers the story. A constant mean forecast through the same volatility-managed rule already achieves a Sharpe of 0.65, and a fixed 0.57× SPY exposure matches buy-and-hold’s Sharpe at much lower volatility: most of the strategy’s character comes from volatility management and lower average market exposure, not from the twelve features. A CAPM regression gives an annualized alpha of 1.8% with a Newey–West t-statistic of only 0.59 — positive but statistically indistinguishable from zero. We therefore do not claim a robust market-timing alpha; the defensible findings are that shrinkage stabilizes a collinear weak-signal problem, that volatility-managed sizing reshapes the risk profile exactly as advertised (volatility cut by a third, drawdown more than halved), and that cost awareness changes model rankings.

The report follows the project brief: markets and timing (§2), labels and features (§3), benchmarks (§4), model choice (§5), data split (§6), training (§7), validation (§8), the trading rule and costs (§9), out-of-sample evaluation (§10), attribution (§11), interpretation (§12), and reproducibility (§13).

2. Markets, assets, timing, and data sources (brief §4.1)

Asset. A single, highly liquid asset: the SPDR S&P 500 ETF (SPY, NYSE Arca). SPY’s depth makes a 1 bp cost assumption and the absence of market-impact modeling defensible at our position sizes (notional capped at 1.5× equity), and a single-asset design keeps the whole pipeline transparent.

Frequency and trading times. Daily. The decision is made after the close of day t, using only information available up to and including that close. Because the closing price and the day’s full OHLCV are not knowable before the close, we do not assume execution at that same close: the order is executed at the open of day t + 1 (the first genuinely executable price), and the position is held from open t + 1 to open t + 2. The label in §3 is built to match this holding period exactly.

Calendar and currency. US exchange calendar (NYSE trading days), USD throughout, no FX considerations.

Investable benchmark. Buy-and-hold SPY, plus the forecast-based and risk-matched benchmarks of §4.

Data sources (lawful, public).

Series Source Details
SPY daily OHLCV Yahoo Finance chart API (via requests; yfinance as fallback) split- and dividend-adjusted (total-return) OHLC
3-month T-bill rate FRED series DTB3; Yahoo ^IRX (13-week T-bill yield) as fallback annualized percent, converted to a daily rate

Yahoo’s adjusted series is backward-adjusted: historical prices are multiplied by the product of split and dividend factors so returns are continuous across corporate actions. Our fetch script (code/fetch_data.py) pulls the adjusted close together with raw OHLC, rescales the OHLC block by the same adjustment factor, and runs verification checks per the Appendix B checklist of the brief: no duplicated dates, no non-positive prices, and zero days with |rt| > 25% over 5,641 observations (2004-06-01 to 2026-10-30); in addition, a per-event check on the two most recent cash dividends confirms that the vendor’s adjusted return on each ex-date matches the manual total return (Pex + D)/Pprev − 1 to within 3 × 10−5 (residual from cent-level rounding). The data-safety logic downloads and validates both series before atomically replacing the cache, so a failed download can never corrupt a valid one. The risk-free leg of the cached run uses Yahoo ^IRX because the FRED endpoint was unreachable from our network at fetch time; the two quote the same instrument, and the script tries FRED first on every run. All analysis uses historical data only; no live trading of any kind is involved.

3. Labels and features (brief §4.2)

Label. The prediction target is the excess return over the actual holding period. With execution at open t + 1 and a one-day holding period,

yt = Opent + 2/Opent + 1 − 1, label in excess form: yt − rf, t,

where rf, t is the daily T-bill accrual known at close t. Features at close t therefore always predict a return that starts strictly after the decision, and the strategy’s realized P&L in §9–§10 is computed over exactly these open-to-open intervals. We model the excess return so that the label, the position rule (a mean-variance ratio of expected excess return to variance), and the Sharpe accounting all use one consistent quantity.

Features. Twelve features, all computable at the close of day t, in four groups.

Feature Definition Lookback Why it might help
ret_1 close-to-close rt 1 d daily reversal/momentum
mom_5, mom_10, mom_21 Pt/Pt − k − 1 1–4 wk short-horizon trend
vol_5, vol_21 rolling std of r 1 wk / 1 mo volatility state; risk scaling
ma_ratio_10/50/200 Pt/MAk − 1 2 wk–10 mo distance from trend; overextension
rsi_14 14-day RSI 2 wk classic overbought/oversold gauge
range_hl (Ht − Lt)/Pt 1 d intraday stress / realized range
vol_chg log Vt − 21-day mean of log V 1 mo abnormal attention / volume

Momentum/reversal features are motivated by the literature on short-horizon return autocorrelation (liquidity-provision reversal at daily horizons). Volatility features capture the persistence of the variance process, used both as predictor and as the denominator of the position rule. Trend-deviation features proxy for how stretched price is relative to its recent path; range/volume proxy for intraday stress and attention.

Known at decision time. Every feature is a deterministic function of OHLCV at or before close t; row t (features) predicts the open t + 1 → open t + 2 return. No quantity revealed after the decision close is used — including for execution, which is deferred to the next open.

Scaling. Features are z-scored with mean and standard deviation fitted on the training window only, then applied unchanged to validation and (after refitting on train+validation) to test. The scaler never sees the period it is applied to.

Cleaning. SPY is clean and liquid; we (i) drop rows with missing OHLCV, (ii) verify no duplicated dates and no non-positive prices, (iii) verify no single-day moves beyond ±25% (none occur), and (iv) drop the initial 200 trading days consumed by the longest lookback (plus the two trailing days consumed by the label). No winsorization: extreme days such as the COVID crash are genuine market data that the risk scaling should react to, not outliers to erase. Missing risk-free quotes (holidays) are forward-filled.

4. Benchmarks (brief §4.3)

Five references, all over the identical test dates and — where applicable — the identical position rule and cost model:

  1. Buy-and-hold SPY — the investable alternative a real investor could hold.
  2. AR(1) forecast baseline — OLS of the excess label on ret_1 only, refit on the same training data, put through the same volatility-managed position rule with its own training-calibrated risk scaler. Isolates the value of the multivariate ridge signal over the simplest time-series forecast.
  3. Mean forecast + volatility management — a constant forecast (the training mean excess return) through the identical position rule. This is the key attribution benchmark: any gap between the ridge strategy and this row is the incremental value of the twelve features; the row itself captures the value of volatility-managed sizing alone.
  4. Fixed-exposure benchmark (exploratory) — a constant SPY weight equal to the ridge strategy’s average test exposure (0.568), with the same cash accounting. Because it is calibrated on test information, it is reported as an exploratory diagnostic only: it checks whether the strategy’s risk profile simply reflects holding less market.
  5. Recursive historical mean — the expanding mean of the excess label using only labels fully realized by close t (labels through t − 2), the Campbell–Thompson style naive reference for ROOS2 = 1 − ∑(yt − ŷt)2/∑(yt − ȳt)2.

5. Model choice and overfitting control (brief §4.4)

Economic story. At the daily horizon the market’s conditional expected excess return is driven by many weak forces at once: liquidity-driven reversal after large moves, short trend continuation, the volatility state, and how stretched prices are relative to trend. No single feature is reliable — daily predictability is famously faint — but a weighted combination of weak, correlated signals may still carry information, provided the estimation noise in the weights is controlled.

Why ridge. The twelve features are highly collinear (momentum, moving-average, and RSI features all load on recent returns). OLS on collinear features has large coefficient variance, which at daily signal-to-noise ratios turns into unstable forecasts and — after position sizing — wasteful turnover. Ridge adds an ℓ2 penalty λ∥β∥2 that pulls coefficients toward zero, trading a small bias for a large variance reduction. This is the classic bias–variance trade-off: with many correlated weak predictors and a low signal-to-noise ratio, shrinking the coefficients reduces estimation error and stabilizes both the forecast and the resulting trades. The dense-weight assumption of ridge (many small contributions) matches our story better than a sparse selector, which suits settings where only a handful of predictors matter at a time (the one-minute cross-sectional setting of Chinco, Clark-Joseph, and Ye, 2019).

Scope note on the “virtue of complexity”. Kelly, Malamud, and Zhou (2024) show that expected out-of-sample timing performance can rise with model complexity under proper shrinkage — but their result is proved in a high-dimensional regime (thousands of generated features, dense roughly isotropic signals, sufficient mixing). Our model has twelve hand-picked, highly correlated features and ~2,500 training observations, which is decidedly not that regime, and we do not claim our results validate their theory. What our experiment does support is the narrower, standard point: under low signal-to-noise and collinearity, ridge shrinkage reduces forecast and trading instability relative to unpenalized estimation (see the validation curve in §8). We deliberately do not add random features or a nonlinear model to chase the high-complexity regime: with ~2,500 training days the added flexibility would mostly fit noise, and the linear model keeps the coefficient table (§12) directly interpretable.

Overfitting control. Four mechanisms, layered:

  1. Shrinkage, with λ selected out of sample on validation (§8), not in sample.
  2. A small, fixed feature set — twelve interpretable features chosen once, before any fitting.
  3. A single chronological train/validation/test split, used in order (§6).
  4. Position caps and volatility scaling — even a wrong forecast cannot produce an unbounded bet: |πt| ≤ 1.5, and positions shrink automatically when realized variance rises (§9).

6. Data period, split, and test discipline (brief §4.5)

The raw download covers 2004-06-01 to 2026-10-30; after the 200-day lookback features and the two-day-ahead label, the usable sample is 2005-03-18 to 2026-10-28 (5,435 observations), split chronologically:

Split Period (actual decision dates) Days Used for
Training 2005-03-18 – 2014-12-30 2,463 fit scaler, ridge, AR(1); calibrate γ
Validation 2015-01-02 – 2019-12-30 1,257 select λ (the only hyperparameter)
Test 2020-01-02 – 2026-10-28 1,715 confirmatory out-of-sample evaluation

Leakage control. Nothing fitted on later data is used on earlier data: (i) the standardizer is fitted on the training window (train+validation for the final model) and applied forward; (ii) the recursive naive forecast uses only labels fully realized by the decision date (through t − 2); (iii) γ is calibrated on the fitting sample only; (iv) the label starts at the next day’s open, so no same-close execution assumption is involved. In addition, boundary rows: because the label of decision day t is only realized at the open of t + 2, every fit and model-selection sample is restricted to rows whose stored label_end_date is on or before the first out-of-sample decision date — this removes exactly one boundary row per split (2014-12-31 from training, 2019-12-31 from validation and from the final fitting sample), enforced by assertions in code/run_backtest.py::split_dataset and by code/test_backtest.py.

Test discipline — confirmatory vs exploratory analysis. The confirmatory analysis is a single evaluation of the frozen configuration (features, λ from validation, γ, position rule, 1 bp cost) on the test window. After seeing those results we ran exploratory diagnostics — the fixed-exposure benchmark, CAPM attribution, and the cost/cap/long-only variants of Appendix D — to understand where performance comes from. These diagnostics use test information and are labelled exploratory wherever they appear; none was used to re-select the model, and we do not describe the test window as “untouched”. Had we changed the strategy on the basis of these diagnostics, the same period could no longer serve as an honest test set.

7. Training (brief §4.6)

Method. Ridge is fitted in closed form, β̂ = (X⊤X + λI)−1X⊤y, via sklearn.linear_model.Ridge (unpenalized intercept, pre-standardized features), with the excess label as the target. The AR(1) baseline is OLS via sklearn.linear_model.LinearRegression on ret_1. Training is deterministic up to floating-point order.

Protocol. For validation, scaler and ridge are fitted on 2005–2014 and scored on 2015–2019 for each λ in the grid. For the final test, scaler and ridge are refitted on 2005–2019 with the selected λ, then frozen.

Reproducibility. Seed 42 is fixed (the pipeline is deterministic; the seed is set defensively). Versions recorded in output/results/run_config.json: Python 3.14.6, numpy 2.5.3, pandas 3.0.6, scikit-learn 1.9.1. The grid, selected λ, fitted coefficients, γ values, caps, and cost are stored in the same file; every number in this report regenerates via the commands in §13.

8. Validation and model selection (brief §4.7)

Method. A single time-respecting forward split: fit on 2005–2014, evaluate on 2015–2019. Forward validation respects time order — unlike random-fold cross-validation it never fits on the future to predict the past — and mimics live use (fit on the past, apply to the next period).

Search. A small, pre-set grid of ten ridge penalties, λ ∈ {0.1, 1, 10, 100, 103, 3 × 103, 104, 3 × 104, 105, 3 × 105} — ten models tried in total, nothing else tuned. Selection rule (defined in advance): the λ with the highest validation Sharpe of the net-of-cost strategy.

Figure 4. Validation-based model selection. Left axis: validation strategy Sharpe (net of costs). Right axis: validation OOS R2 vs the recursive-mean naive forecast. Strong shrinkage dominates; the Sharpe-optimal penalty is λ = 3 × 104.

Lightly penalized models (λ ≤ 100) are worse than doing nothing on validation (Sharpe ≈ 0 or negative, ROOS2 < 0): without shrinkage the collinear features produce noisy forecasts, and the position rule converts noise into turnover and costs. Performance rises monotonically with λ up to λ = 3 × 104 (validation Sharpe 0.76, annualized return 11.1% at 13.8% volatility) and flattens beyond. This is the textbook bias–variance trade-off: with weak collinear predictors, heavy shrinkage is the main defence against overfitting. The selected model is λ = 3 × 104, an interior optimum of the grid.

9. From predictions to positions (brief §4.8)

Score-to-weight rule. The excess-return forecast is converted into a position by a volatility-managed (mean-variance style) rule:

πt = clip( ŷt / ( γ ⋅ σ̂21, t2 ), −1.5, +1.5 ),

where ŷt is the ridge forecast of the holding-day excess return and σ̂21, t2 is the 21-day realized variance known at close t. This is the mean-variance/Kelly logic (Moreira–Muir style volatility management): position proportional to expected excess return, inversely proportional to variance. The risk-aversion analogue γ is calibrated on the training data only so that the average absolute pre-clip position |ŷ/(γσ̂2)| equals 1 there; the ±1.5 cap is applied afterwards, so the realized mean |π| can be below 1 (γridge = 7.92, γAR(1) = 10.66, γmean = 8.78). γ is then held fixed out of sample. The ±1.5 cap bounds leverage at 1.5× and permits shorts — a simple, stated risk limit.

Cash, financing, and short accounting. The position πt is the fraction of equity in SPY; the remaining 1 − πt earns the T-bill rate. Leverage above 1× borrows at the T-bill rate; short positions invest the proceeds at the T-bill rate. The portfolio return over the holding day is therefore

rp, t = rf, t + πt (yt − rf, t) − 10−4 |πt − πt − 1|.

For SPY — one of the easiest-to-borrow securities in the world — the stock-borrow fee is a few basis points per year and is neglected; this is stated as an assumption rather than modelled.

Execution. Orders are executed at the open of day t + 1 (the first price after the signal is computable); positions are held to the open of t + 2 and rebalanced at each open. The label, the backtest P&L, and this execution rule are aligned by construction.

Costs. Daily strategies cannot assume costs away: we charge 1 bp (10−4) per unit of target-weight turnover |πt − πt − 1|. This is a deliberately simple proxy, not an exact fill model: it ignores the weight drift that market moves induce between rebalances, intraday slippage against the open print, market impact, and the terminal liquidation of the final position. For SPY at our sizes these are second-order, and Appendix D shows the test Sharpe degrades gracefully (0.67 / 0.64 / 0.60 at 0.5 / 1 / 2 bp). For transparent comparison, the buy-and-hold benchmark is charged no costs at all — a single entry trade — which is generous to the benchmark, not to the strategy. Turnover and the resulting drag are reported explicitly in §10.

Risk discipline. The ±1.5 cap; automatic de-risking via the σ̂212 denominator in volatile regimes; and turnover implicitly penalized through the cost term, which makes faint forecasts uneconomic to trade.

10. Out-of-sample evaluation (brief §4.9)

Train–test correspondence. Everything the confirmatory test evaluation uses was frozen before the test window: the feature definitions, the scaler (fitted on 2005–2019), the ridge coefficients (λ = 3 × 104 chosen on 2015–2019), γ, the position rule and cap, and the 1 bp cost. At each test date we compute features after close t, forecast the excess return, set πt, execute at open t + 1, earn rf + πt(yt − rf) minus turnover costs over the holding day, and move forward. The test window 2020-01-02 to 2026-10-28 (1,715 days: COVID crash, 2022 bear market, 2023–2026 bull run) received exactly one confirmatory evaluation.

11. Attribution: where does the performance come from?

A strategy can beat its benchmark by holding less market, by managing exposure over time, or by forecasting returns. These have very different economic meanings, so we decompose the ridge strategy’s test performance against the benchmarks of §4.

Exposure. The ridge strategy holds SPY 88.5% of days with an average position of 0.568 — it is, on average, roughly half invested. A fixed 0.568× SPY weight with the same cash accounting earns a Sharpe of 0.69 — matching buy-and-hold (0.69) at barely over half the volatility. So at this horizon, simply holding less market already reproduces the headline Sharpe; the low drawdown of the timing strategy is largely a low-exposure effect.

Volatility management. The mean-forecast row isolates the position rule: a constant bullish forecast, scaled each day by 1/σ̂212, achieves Sharpe 0.65 with the lowest drawdown of any row (−9.7%) and almost no turnover (0.04/day, 0.10%/yr drag). Volatility-managed sizing by itself explains most of the strategy’s character — cutting exposure in high-variance regimes (2020, 2022) and restoring it in calm ones.

Forecasting increment. What the twelve features add on top is modest: ridge return is higher than the mean-forecast row (10.5% vs 8.8%) but with higher volatility, for an essentially identical Sharpe (0.64 vs 0.65); and the forecast ROOS2 is 0.0010 — positive against the recursive mean, but economically tiny.

CAPM. Regressing the strategy’s daily net excess return on the market excess return (Newey–West t-statistics, lag 5):

Model α (ann.) β t(α) t(β)
Ridge strategy +1.8% 0.47 0.59 11.59
Mean fcst + vol mgmt +1.6% 0.34 0.66 9.71
AR(1) strategy +0.9% 0.35 0.23 8.17

The market beta of 0.47 confirms the low-exposure character. The alpha is positive but its t-statistic of 0.59 is far from significant — the data do not reject “no alpha”. Notably, the mean-forecast benchmark shows a similar insignificant alpha, consistent with volatility management (a real, documented effect — Moreira and Muir, 2017) rather than return prediction driving both.

What we conclude. The strategy’s out-of-sample behavior is explained primarily by (i) lower average market exposure and (ii) volatility-managed sizing; the incremental contribution of the twelve technical features is small and not statistically established. We do not claim a robust timing alpha.

12. Interpretation: what worked, what did not

What the model learned. Fitted coefficients (standardized features, 2005–2019) are dominated by short-horizon reversal: ret_1 (−7.2×10⁻⁵), mom_5 (−9.4×10⁻⁵), mom_10 (−5.0×10⁻⁵), ma_ratio_10 (−8.7×10⁻⁵) all carry negative weights — after up-moves and above-trend prices the model expects give-back. Volatility features carry small negative weights; range_hl (+3.6×10⁻⁵) is small positive; the intercept (+3.6×10⁻⁴ ≈ 9.6%/yr) is the unconditional excess premium. Every coefficient is small — shrinkage spreads the signal thinly across all twelve features.

What worked. (i) Shrinkage: the single most important design choice — validation Sharpe went from ≈ 0 at λ ≤ 100 to 0.76 at λ = 3 × 104, and the edge survived into the test window. (ii) Volatility-managed sizing: it, not the forecast, produced the attractive risk profile, as the mean-forecast benchmark makes visible (Sharpe 0.65, max DD −9.7% standalone). (iii) Cost-aware evaluation: it changed rankings — AR(1) looks acceptable gross and is clearly inferior net. (iv) Executable timing: moving execution to the next open removed the same-close look-ahead that a close t signal cannot honestly trade on. (v) Process discipline: one confirmatory test evaluation, with all later diagnostics clearly fenced off as exploratory.

What did not work. (i) Beating buy-and-hold in a bull market: the strategy underperforms on raw return and slightly on Sharpe over 2020–2026; partial exposure forgoes upside in persistent uptrends. (ii) Demonstrating forecast value: the ridge forecast’s ROOS2 of 0.0010 is statistically and economically faint, the CAPM alpha is insignificant, and the features add no Sharpe over volatility-managed sizing alone — an honest negative result about daily technical predictability, not a model failure. (iii) AR(1) as a benchmark: its negative test ROOS2 confirms that the simplest autoregressive signal does not survive contact with costs.

Limitations. Single asset (no cross-sectional diversification of signal noise); a single train/validation/test split; flat 1 bp cost with no market-impact or borrow-fee modeling; linear model only; no statistical significance claimed for the forecast itself. Natural extensions, in order: expanding-window periodic refits, and a small cross-section of index ETFs to diversify signal noise. We deliberately did not add model complexity (random features, neural networks): the binding constraint is the faint daily signal, not functional flexibility, and a larger search would raise the risk of test-set reuse.

Appendix C. Data dictionary

Raw files in data/ (produced by code/fetch_data.py):

File Columns Notes
spy_daily.csv date, open, high, low, close, volume split/dividend-adjusted OHLCV, 2004-06-01 → 2026-10-30, 5,641 rows
dtb3.csv date, dtb3 annualized 3-month T-bill rate in percent (FRED DTB3, or Yahoo ^IRX fallback)

Derived panel (built in memory by code/features.py::build_dataset): one row per decision date t from 2005-03-18 to 2026-10-28 (5,435 rows), with the twelve features of §3, the 21-day realized variance σ̂21, t2, the open-to-open label fwd_ret (Opent + 2/Opent + 1 − 1), label_end_date (the date the label is fully realized — the open of t + 2 — used to exclude unrealized labels at split boundaries), the risk-free accrual rf_next, the excess label fwd_excess, and the recursive naive forecast naive_excess (expanding mean of fwd_excess through t − 2). No derived data is persisted; everything is rebuilt from the raw files on every run.

Appendix D. Robustness diagnostics (exploratory)

The following variants were computed after the single confirmatory test evaluation, as diagnostics of how sensitive the conclusions are to design knobs. They use test information, were not used to re-select anything, and the submitted configuration remains the pre-set one (λ from the validation grid, cap ±1.5, 1 bp cost, long/short). Every number below is generated by code/run_robustness.py from the frozen main configuration and stored in output/results/robustness.csv.

  1. Cost sensitivity. Test Sharpe is 0.67 at 0.5 bp, 0.64 at the 1 bp baseline, and 0.60 at 2 bp per unit turnover: the ridge strategy degrades gracefully because its turnover is low. The AR(1) baseline (3× higher turnover) degrades much faster, so the ranking ridge > AR(1) is unchanged at every cost level.
  2. Position cap. Capping at ±1.0 instead of ±1.5 leaves the test Sharpe essentially unchanged (0.66 vs 0.64; annualized return 9.7% vs 10.5%): the strategy does not depend on the leverage allowance.
  3. Long-only variant. Clipping positions to [0, 1.5] gives a test Sharpe of 0.64 (vs 0.64 with shorts): most of the value comes from scaling exposure down in high-variance regimes, not from being short.
